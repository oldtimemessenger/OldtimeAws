1. How Instagram subscriptions actually work

Instagram subscriptions allow eligible creators to charge their followers a recurring monthly fee in exchange for exclusive content and experiences.

According to Meta’s published descriptions, its subscription features have included exclusive Stories, Reels, posts, subscriber badges, and subscriber-only chats.  

Here’s how it works from both sides.

A. The creator sets up a subscription

Imagine a creator named Alex has 50,000 followers.

Alex opens their professional creator dashboard and enables subscriptions.

Alex chooses a monthly price, such as:

* $0.99/month
* $2.99/month
* $4.99/month
* $9.99/month

Those are example prices for our explanation, not a claim about Instagram’s currently available price options.

Alex then decides what paying subscribers receive.

For example:

Feature	Free followers	Paying subscribers
Regular posts	Yes	Yes
Public Reels	Yes	Yes
Regular Stories	Yes	Yes
Exclusive posts	No	Yes
Exclusive Reels	No	Yes
Subscriber-only Stories	No	Yes
Special subscriber badge	No	Yes
Subscriber-only community chats	No	Yes
Exclusive broadcasts	No	Yes, if offered

The creator still has a public audience. Subscriptions create an additional layer of access rather than replacing the public profile.

B. What the fan sees

A fan visits Alex’s profile.

They see a button:

Subscribe — $4.99/month

They tap it, review the benefits, and complete the purchase.

After the subscription becomes active:

* The fan receives a subscriber badge.
* Subscriber-only content becomes accessible.
* Exclusive posts appear in the appropriate feed or profile section.
* The fan can join eligible subscriber-only experiences.
* The subscription renews automatically unless canceled, subject to the applicable billing terms.

The creator earns recurring revenue from active subscriptions.

That’s the powerful part: the creator doesn’t have to convince the same fan to make a new purchase every day.

One subscription can generate income month after month.

Hundreds of people are watching.

Most people are commenting normally, but one fan wants Alex to notice their message.

They purchase a Super Chat.

Their message becomes visually prominent in the live chat.

The important distinction: a subscription provides continuing benefits, while a Super Chat is an individual purchase for a moment of recognition or interaction.

That’s why combining them makes sense.

7. The money: How does everyone get paid?

Let’s use a hypothetical example.

Suppose a creator has 1,000 paying members at $4.99 per month.

Their gross monthly membership sales would be:

1,000 × $4.99 = $4,990 per month.

Now suppose fans also send $1,000 worth of gifts during the month.

That produces another $1,000 in gross sales.

Total gross sales:

$5,990 per month.

But this is not the creator’s take-home income.

You must account for:

* Applicable Apple or Google billing fees.
* Payment processing costs.
* Refunds and chargebacks.
* Applicable taxes.
* Your platform’s share.
* Any other agreed deductions.

For illustration, imagine Oldtimehive uses a 20% platform fee on eligible transactions, after which other applicable costs are accounted for separately.

If we temporarily ignore those other costs:

Revenue source	Gross sales	Oldtimehive’s hypothetical 20%	Creator’s remaining 80%
Memberships	$4,990	$998	$3,992
Gifts	$1,000	$200	$800
Total	$5,990	$1,198	$4,792

These are illustrative figures, not a finalized payout policy or a promise of net earnings.

The actual calculation must account for the billing channel and applicable fees.

For comparison, YouTube’s published Super Chat revenue-share explanation says eligible creators receive 70% of confirmed Supers revenue after applicable local sales taxes and iOS App Store fees are deducted.  

You can choose a different model for Oldtimehive, provided it is economically sustainable and clearly disclosed.

My recommendation: start by evaluating a 15–20% platform fee on eligible creator transactions, then model payment costs and mobile-store requirements before deciding.

Don’t promise creators an 80% net payout if the 80% figure is actually calculated before other deductions.

8. Do you need Stripe for this?

Yes, a payment provider will be important, but there’s a detail we need to get right.

You previously said you’re building Oldtimehive as a mobile-first web app and want to move away from Replit.

For that architecture, Stripe could be useful for web-based creator memberships and eligible purchases.

However, if you later distribute the product as an iOS or Android app, the rules for digital purchases can differ by platform, storefront, transaction type, and applicable exceptions.

You cannot assume that every in-app digital membership or virtual gift can use Stripe instead of Apple or Google billing.

Before building the final purchase flow, you should review Apple’s current rules in the  Apple App Review Guidelines⁠￼ and Google’s  Google Play payments policy⁠￼.

I’d separate the architecture into three pieces:

1. Purchase interface: The screen where fans choose a membership or gift.
2. Payment integration: The approved billing method for that transaction and platform.
3. Entitlement and earnings system: The server verifies payment, grants access, records the transaction, and calculates the creator’s share.

This is important because your app should not grant paid membership access just because the frontend displays a successful-looking payment screen.

The server must verify the transaction.

10. One critical decision: coins or direct purchases?

You have two main choices for gifts.

Option A: Direct purchases

A fan purchases a specific gift for $2.99.

The transaction is recorded, and the creator earns the applicable share.

Advantages:

* Easier for users to understand the cost.
* More transparent purchase records.
* No separate virtual-currency balance to manage.

Disadvantages:

* Every gift requires its own purchase flow unless the platform supports another approved method.
* Repeated small transactions can create friction.

Option B: A virtual-currency wallet

Fans buy a pack of Oldtimehive coins or another virtual currency.

For example:

Pack	Example price
100 coins	$0.99
350 coins	$2.99
700 coins	$4.99
1,500 coins	$9.99

These are illustrative prices and quantities.

Fans then spend those coins on gifts.

Advantages:

* Faster gifting.
* More flexible gift animations and pricing.
* Easier to encourage repeat interactions.

Disadvantages:

* More complicated accounting and refunds.
* Currency balances and spending must be secured.
* You must define expiration, refund, and redemption rules.
* Virtual currency may trigger additional platform requirements.

For your app, I’d lean toward a virtual-currency system for gifts, with memberships purchased separately.

That gives you a clean distinction:

* Memberships buy continuing access.
* Coins buy virtual gifts.
* Creator earnings are calculated and tracked separately.

But don’t make coins redeemable for cash by ordinary users. Creator earnings and fan currency should be separate ledgers with clearly defined rules.
